CASE_01 Payments Infrastructure

Payment connectivity & settlement platform.

A top-5 US bank's payment-processing engine was producing 12% error rates on monthly commission payouts — quietly costing $2M+ per quarter in reconciliation work and eroding agent trust.

The problem
Six teams, one rule engine, no shared source of truth. Payment logic lived across three systems with subtle inconsistencies. Each month-end produced a backlog of disputes, reconciliations, and one-off corrections. Engineering wanted to rewrite from scratch; Operations wanted stability; Finance wanted the errors to stop yesterday. My job was to design a path through that respected all three — without breaking idempotency, audit trails, or settlement timing along the way.
My approach
Started with a 4-week discovery: shadowed 12 commission analysts, mapped the rule logic across all three systems, and built a unified business-rules dictionary. Sequenced the program into three releases — stabilize the most-disputed rules first, migrate calculation to a single event-driven engine second, deprecate legacy paths last. Partnered with engineering on the architecture: AWS API Gateway as the edge, Lambda for rule evaluation, EventBridge for state transitions, SQS for retries with idempotency keys. The hardest call was a one-way-door decision on the rule-engine architecture — I documented the trade-offs and brought it to the sponsor with a clear recommendation, not a shrug.

CASE_02 AI/ML Platform

Risk
Moderate — hallucination cost, adversarial inputs

AI agent platform & evaluation framework.

Production AI agents serving 12 enterprise customers had no evaluation rigor — prompt-iteration was reactive, regressions surfaced in customer feedback, and there was no shared definition of "agent quality."

The problem
AI agents were shipping based on vibes. Every prompt change was a roll of the dice — improve one cohort, silently break another. Engineering wanted a quality bar before adding more agents to the platform. The customer-facing teams wanted faster iteration. Nobody had a shared answer to a basic question: "Is this version better than the last one, and by how much?"
My approach
Built the evaluation framework with three layers: golden datasets (curated, versioned, owned by domain experts), automated regression tests (run on every prompt change), and online A/B testing (with statistical-significance thresholds before rolling out). Defined the agent-quality KPIs we'd commit to: precision, recall on harmful content, hallucination rate, latency P95, cost-per-call. Partnered with engineering on the tooling: prompt versioning, eval harnesses, structured logging. Each agent feature now ships with its own evaluation suite before it touches production.

03 / Operating Principles

Six rules I work by.

/ 01
Design for the edges
The happy path is rarely the problem. R10 returns, wire-transfer reversals, exception queues, and dead-letter flows are where customer trust and audit-trail integrity actually get earned. I design for the edges first.
/ 02
Be a peer to engineering, not a translator
If I can't reason about distributed systems, idempotency, and exception handling in the same room as the engineers, I'm not actually doing technical PM work. I keep my technical depth current as a deliberate practice.
/ 03
Hold the safety bar
Platforms earn trust by what they refuse to compromise on. Settlement accuracy. Audit-trail integrity. Idempotency contracts. I write these down explicitly so they don't get traded away under deadline pressure.
/ 04
Reduce friction for consumers, not just users
The teams that consume my platform are my customers too. Standardized SDKs, idempotency-key conventions, exception-handling contracts — these are products in their own right, and I treat them that way.
/ 05
Model second-order effects
Every platform decision flows downstream. A change to settlement timing changes Treasury reporting. A change to retry logic changes operational load. I model what happens two and three steps out — and write it down so the team can challenge it.
/ 06
Ship — then iterate
The work isn't done until it's in production and being used. I'd rather ship a smaller v1 with clear KPIs and an iteration plan than a perfect v2 that arrives a quarter late. Four consecutive on-time releases isn't a happy accident.
